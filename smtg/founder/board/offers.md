# Offers lens
Score: 4 / 10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong

**The pain is real and recurring.** Most households own dozens of items used
once or twice a year. The gap between "I need a drill for one afternoon" and
"I should buy a $120 drill" is felt by millions of people, every day. The
pain is not manufactured.

**The dream outcome scores well.** For the renter: get the thing you need in
under an hour, pay almost nothing, return it and forget it. That is a high
dream-outcome score — it maps cleanly onto one of the four value-equation
dimensions. For the owner: earn passive income from something sitting idle.
Both sides have a genuine outcome to sell.

**The effort ask on the renter is low in principle.** Browse, request, pay,
pick up, return. Fewer steps than buying. The model is structurally suited
to low-friction delivery if the app is built well.

**Commission-only pricing is correct at this stage.** No listing fee removes
the first barrier for supply. Owners have nothing to lose by listing. That
is the right call when the cold-start problem is the existential risk.

## What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)

**1. Perceived likelihood of success is near zero without density.**
The value equation's most dangerous lever here is not the dream outcome —
it is the buyer's belief that they will actually get it. A renter opens the
app, searches for a pressure washer within 3 km, and finds nothing. They
close the app and never return. This is not a product problem; it is a
marketplace density problem, and it hits before the product can demonstrate
its value at all. Without a credible answer to "how do we get to 50
listings in one neighbourhood before we market to renters," every other
strength is irrelevant. The brief names this as an open question. It is the
primary kill condition.

**2. The damage/trust problem collapses the offer before it starts.**
The brief flags this explicitly and correctly. No deposit, no insurance, no
dispute resolution — these are not features to add later. They are the
reason a rational owner does not list their pressure washer for $15/day. A
$400 item rented for $15 with no recourse if it comes back broken is not a
passive income opportunity; it is an unpriced insurance policy the owner is
writing. The offer to owners cannot close without an answer to this. The
answer (deposit structure, damage waiver, third-party insurance, platform
guarantee) is also a major cost and complexity item. Until it is designed
and costed, the owner-side value proposition is conceptual. The
`founder-cfo` skill should model the unit economics once the protection
mechanism is chosen, because the commission rate that makes sense under a
1% damage rate looks very different under a 5% one.

**3. Supply-side quality and pricing are uncontrolled, making the renter
experience unreliable.**
Owners set their own prices and decide their own availability windows. The
platform has no lever on item quality, photo quality, response time, or
price reasonableness. The renter's experience is entirely dependent on the
weakest owner in their search radius. This is solvable with ratings, with
onboarding guidance, with suggested pricing — but none of that is in the
brief, and all of it costs product and ops time. Without it, early word of
mouth is determined by the worst interactions, not the average ones. The
effort-and-sacrifice dimension of the value equation rises fast when renters
get ghosted, receive items in poor condition, or find listed prices 3x what
they expected.

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. **What is the first neighbourhood, and why that one?** Not the city —
   the specific walkable or driveable radius where you will get to 50 listings
   before you spend a dollar on renter acquisition. What is your plan to get
   there, and how long does it take?

2. **What happens when an item comes back damaged?** Walk me through the
   exact resolution flow: who pays, how fast, what documentation is required,
   and what the platform covers. If you do not have this answer, you do not
   yet have an offer to owners.

3. **What does the owner actually earn on a typical item?**
   This is the open number that drives everything on the supply side. A $20
   drill rented 4 times a year at $8/day, with a 15% commission and a 2%
   expected damage rate, produces a specific net figure. That figure either
   makes listing worth the hassle or it does not. Has anyone done this math
   with real categories? The `founder-cfo` skill answers this.

4. **What is the total addressable market in the launch city, and what
   commission volume is needed to reach sustainability?**
   Market size is an open question the brief acknowledges. The `founder-cfo`
   skill builds this model; the `founder-competitors` skill maps who is
   already doing this locally (Fat Llama, Loanables, and others have tried
   versions of this). That competitive picture changes the market-size
   interpretation.

5. **Why would someone choose this over Facebook Marketplace or a neighbour
   they already know?** The trust and convenience delta needs to be named
   precisely. "It's easier" is not an answer. The specific friction the
   platform removes — payment safety, discovery radius, item availability
   calendar — needs to be the offer's headline.

6. **What is the guarantee?**
   The Offers lens asks this directly: if the renter does not find what they
   need, or the item is not as described, what does the platform promise and
   deliver? A marketplace with no guarantee is asking buyers to take all the
   risk. What is the platform willing to put behind the transaction?

## If FUND IF: the conditions

The score of 4 reflects the business at its current stage of definition —
ideation only, with the two hardest structural questions (density and trust)
unanswered. The vote is FUND IF rather than PASS because the pain is real,
the market exists, and peer-to-peer rental has worked in adjacent categories
(cars, accommodation, power tools in some markets). None of that is
guaranteed to transfer here, but it is not a structurally broken model.

The conditions are:

**Condition 1: A trust and protection mechanism is designed, costed, and
stress-tested before launch.**
This means: a specific deposit structure or insurance product is chosen; the
unit economics are modelled with damage rates included; and the resolution
flow for a damaged or missing item is written out end to end. This is not
optional detail — it is the owner-side offer. Without it there is no supply,
and without supply there is no marketplace. The `founder-cfo` skill should
run this model once the mechanism is chosen.

**Condition 2: A cold-start plan names a specific first neighbourhood and
a specific path to 50 listings before renter marketing begins.**
The plan should name: who the first 50 owners are, how they are reached
(direct outreach, community groups, existing networks), and what the
founder's unfair advantage is in that neighbourhood. A city-level TAM is
not a cold-start plan.

**Condition 3: Competitive mapping is done before the offer is finalised.**
Fat Llama, Loanables, and local Facebook/NextDoor rental behaviour are the
real comparison set. The `founder-competitors` skill runs this. If a
well-funded competitor has already tried and exited this exact model in a
comparable city, that is a material signal about the offer's viability —
not necessarily fatal, but it changes what needs to be true.

If all three conditions are met with credible specifics, the score moves to
6-7 and the vote moves to FUND. The underlying pain is real enough to
justify continued investment in validating the model.
